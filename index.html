Letter · Head of MPD
Dear Staff Member,

I am pleased to welcome you to the Monetary Policy Department of the Central Bank of Armenia.

I have no doubt that you already appreciate the importance of the Central Bank’s role in our economy. Nevertheless, I would like to begin by emphasizing its mission: to ensure and strengthen macroeconomic stability and, in doing so, contribute to the continued improvement of society’s wellbeing. I hope this is already a personal goal for you, or will become one over time.

The Monetary Policy Department plays a key role in the Central Bank’s policymaking process by providing the analytical foundation for well-informed and well-grounded decisions by the Board. Through rigorous research, in-depth economic analysis, comprehensive risk assessments, and the integration of diverse professional perspectives, we contribute to effective and forward-looking monetary policy. Our work ultimately supports the Central Bank’s commitment to price stability and is among the most important preconditions for Armenia’s long-term economic development and improving the wellbeing of its citizens.

Like Armenia itself, the Central Bank - and our Monetary Policy team in particular- has faced numerous trials and challenges over the past decades. I am proud to say that throughout this time, we have consistently succeeded in maintaining price stability in Armenia while gradually strengthening the Central Bank’s credibility. These challenges have made us stronger, more experienced, and more confident in our ability to face new challenges that may lie ahead. I believe that the flexibility to evolve with the times and respond to future challenges has been one of the keys to our success. Our team’s continued professional growth and independent thinking have been equally important. I hope that you, too, will help preserve and continually strengthen these important values.

A notable example of the Central Bank’s commitment to analytical excellence, innovation, and collaboration is the Prudent Risk Management Approach to Price Stability, developed and formally introduced in 2024. It is the world’s first framework for monetary policy as risk management. Built on robust analytics and effective Board-staff collaboration, it enables us not only to follow global best practice but also to develop new approaches and set new standards.

We firmly believe that effective policymaking depends not only on strong institutional frameworks but also on people - their knowledge, professional expertise, and new ideas. That is why our professional culture places great emphasis on professional independence, critical thinking, and initiative. We encourage new ideas, the open exchange of different perspectives, and a willingness to continuously revisit and improve established approaches. Mutual trust and collaboration are equally fundamental to our work, as we believe the best outcomes are achieved through collective discussion and effort. And, of course, continuous learning is an essential part of this culture, reflecting our commitment to acquiring new knowledge and developing professional capabilities. By joining our team, you become part of this environment. I am confident that your knowledge, skills, and fresh perspectives will make a valuable contribution to our collective work and support the Department’s continued development. At the same time, I hope that your experience at the Central Bank will provide opportunities for professional growth, learning, and engaging challenges.

To support your seamless transition, we have prepared this onboarding platform. It will help you strengthen your understanding of the evolution of economic thought, Armenia’s economic history, the Central Bank’s mission, the foundational principles of monetary policymaking, and the nuances of our policymaking framework. The roadmap on the platform will guide you through your introductory learning. However, this is only the first step in your professional development. It will be followed by a professional training program and opportunities for continuous learning. I hope the knowledge gained through this platform will provide a valuable foundation for your future work.

I am confident that together we will continue strengthening our analytical and research capabilities, refining our policymaking approaches, and contributing to the Central Bank’s broader vision: to be a leading central bank, driven by research excellence and innovation.

Once again, welcome to our team. I wish you a rewarding and productive experience, continued professional success, and many new achievements.

Welcome to the Monetary Policy Department!

VP
Vazgen Poghosyan
Head of Monetary Policy Department, CBA

01 Policy Imperatives in the Age of Uncertainty 9 min
VA
Vahe Avagyan
Advisor to the Governor, CBA

The world did not simply become more risky after 2020. It became structurally uncertain in ways that exposed a fundamental flaw in how central banks had been thinking about policy. But before examining that flaw, this video asks a more basic question: what is the fundamental role of monetary policy? The answer the CBA arrived at is unambiguous. Monetary policy is not primarily a forecasting exercise. Its fundamental purpose is to prevent the economy from sliding into dark corners, outcomes so adverse and so costly that they cannot be easily reversed. Vahe Avagyan examines the three layers of uncertainty that make this task so demanding: uncertainty about the state of the economy, uncertainty about its structure and the nonlinear relationships that linear models fail to capture, and the decision-making trap these uncertainties create. The temptation to resolve uncertainty by committing to a single baseline does not eliminate uncertainty. It amplifies it, ties the central bank’s hands, and places its credibility at unnecessary risk.

The Role of Scenarios in the Prudent Risk Management Framework

01 Defining Scenarios Under PRMAPS vs. Traditional Frameworks 5 min
HA
Hayk Avetisyan
Director of the Macroeconomic Directorate, CBA

In traditional monetary policy frameworks, scenarios are used to test the sensitivity of a baseline forecast to alternative assumptions. Under PRMAPS, their purpose is fundamentally different. Hayk Avetisyan explains how the Taxonomy of Scenarios defines scenarios as plausible economic and financial developments not currently priced in financial markets, whose purpose is to identify the policy-relevant risks that, if left unmanaged, could result in policy errors and push the economy toward dark corners. These are not deviations from a central view — they are realistic outcomes that cause genuine concern for policymakers, structured within a classification that distinguishes between developments requiring tighter policy and those requiring looser policy, both relative to the Market Reference Scenario. Critically, under PRMAPS it is the Board that develops the full set of scenarios in the Taxonomy of Scenarios, not staff. I. Intellectual foundations and the historical overview
1
Paul A. Samuelson and Robert M. Solow, "Analytical Aspects of Anti-Inflation Policy" (1960)
SOURCE · JSTOR stable article page →

Samuelson and Solow present the influential interpretation of the relationship between unemployment and inflation that preceded the Great Inflation. The original contribution contains qualifications that are often absent from later summaries. It nevertheless clarifies the basis on which policymakers came to view aggregate demand management as providing a durable choice between inflation and employment outcomes.

Recommended focus: The discussion of the observed relationship between inflation and unemployment, together with the concluding policy implications.

2
Milton Friedman, "The Role of Monetary Policy" (1968)
SOURCE · American Economic Association · full article →

Friedman explains why monetary policy cannot maintain unemployment permanently below its sustainable level and why an apparently stable relationship between inflation and unemployment changes as expectations adjust. The article provides a foundation for the modern understanding of inflation expectations, the natural rate and the limitations of discretionary fine tuning.

Recommended focus: The sections on what monetary policy can and cannot accomplish and the discussion of the natural rate.

3
Michael D. Bordo and Athanasios Orphanides, "Introduction," in The Great Inflation: The Rebirth of Modern Central Banking (2013)
SOURCE · NBER · official chapter in full →  ·  SOURCE · volume and contents →

The introduction provides a comprehensive synthesis of the literature. It distinguishes among monetary accommodation, supply shocks, deficient policy doctrine, mismeasurement of economic capacity, institutional pressures and international monetary conditions. The cross country evidence further shows why similar external shocks produced different inflation outcomes.

Recommended focus: The full introduction, pages 1 to 22.

4
John B. Taylor, "The Great Inflation, the Great Disinflation, and Policies for Future Price Stability" (1992)
SOURCE · Reserve Bank of Australia · full conference paper →

Taylor reviews the rise and reversal of inflation across the United States, Germany, Japan and other economies. The paper examines why governments tolerated inflation despite recognizing its costs and relates the historical record to the design of more predictable policy. Taylor was also among the senior economists who advised the development of the CBA's prudent risk management framework.

Recommended focus: Sections 1 and 2 and the concluding historical lessons; the econometric sections may be omitted.

5
Athanasios Orphanides, "The Road to Price Stability" (2006)
SOURCE · Federal Reserve Board · publication page and full paper →

Orphanides reconstructs the transition from Volcker's declaration of war on inflation to the consolidation of price stability under Greenspan. Drawing extensively on the historical record, the paper examines the evolution of policy judgment, communication and commitment rather than relying on a formal model. It complements the overview by Bordo and Orphanides and reflects the work of an economist whose advice is acknowledged in the CBA volume.

Recommended focus: The historical narrative and the concluding discussion of the conditions required to preserve price stability.

6
Guy Debelle and Douglas Laxton, "Is the Phillips Curve Really a Curve? Some Evidence for Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States" (1996)
SOURCE · IMF Working Paper →

Debelle and Laxton test whether the Phillips curve is convex, finding that a given fall in unemployment below the NAIRU raises inflation by more than an equivalent rise in unemployment lowers it. The result implies that a central bank which fails to stabilise the business cycle will induce a permanently higher natural rate of unemployment, reinforcing the case against the discretionary fine tuning that contributed to the Great Inflation. Laxton was later among the senior economists who advised the development of the CBA's prudent risk management framework.

Recommended focus: The statement of the convexity result and its policy implications in the introduction and conclusion; the econometric and NAIRU-filtering sections may be omitted.

7
Charles Freedman and Douglas Laxton, "Why Inflation Targeting?" (2009)
SOURCE · International Monetary Fund · publication page and full paper →

Freedman and Laxton connect the breakdown of the long term Phillips curve relationship to the adoption of a nominal anchor and a full inflation targeting framework. The proposed assignment concerns the institutional argument and practical explanation rather than the formal material. It establishes the intellectual lineage of the framework that preceded FPAS Mark II.

Recommended focus: The sections on the costs of inflation, the need for a nominal anchor and the defining elements of inflation targeting.

II. Firsthand accounts from monetary policymakers
8
FIRSTHAND POLICYMAKER SOURCE
Helmut Schlesinger, "Recent Experiences with Monetary Policy in the Federal Republic of Germany" (1976)
SOURCE · Duncker & Humblot · bilingual article and complete PDF →

Writing as a member of the Bundesbank Directorate during the Great Inflation, Schlesinger explains how the move to floating exchange rates widened the scope for an independent policy directed toward price stability. The German case provides a counterpoint to the United States. Comparable external shocks produced different outcomes under different monetary institutions, policy priorities and responses to inflation.

Recommended focus: The English language account of the policy regime after 1973 and the concluding institutional lessons.

FIRSTHAND POLICYMAKER SOURCE
Arthur F. Burns, "The Anguish of Central Banking" (1979)
SOURCE · Per Jacobsson Foundation · full lecture →

Burns chaired the Federal Reserve during much of the Great Inflation. His lecture describes the political pressures, fiscal conditions, social demands and prevailing ideas that he considered constraints on central bank action. The account shows how recognition of the inflation problem may coexist with an inadequate policy response.

Recommended focus: Burns's diagnosis of institutional constraints and his account of the limits of central bank independence.

FIRSTHAND POLICYMAKER SOURCE

Delivered shortly after the Federal Reserve announced its October 1979 changes to operating procedures, this statement provides a contemporaneous explanation of the regime shift. Volcker addresses inflation expectations, policy credibility, the external value of the dollar, the need for persistence and the complementary role of fiscal discipline before the outcome of the disinflation was known.

Recommended focus: The opening diagnosis, the explanation of the October measures and the concluding discussion of policy persistence.

The account illustrates the exercise of judgment and public responsibility, including the acceptance of criticism in the near term in pursuit of lasting stability.

Recommended focus: The chapters covering Volcker's appointment as Federal Reserve Chair, the October 1979 policy change and the subsequent disinflation.

III. Historical interpretation and comparative cases
18
J. Bradford DeLong, "America's Peacetime Inflation: The 1970s" (1997)
SOURCE · NBER · full chapter →

DeLong's accessible historical account emphasises how the memory of the Great Depression led policymakers to place greater weight on avoiding unemployment than on containing an initially gradual increase in inflation. The chapter combines political history, economic ideas and institutional incentives and illustrates how the lessons of one crisis may distort the response to the next.

Recommended focus: The historical narrative and the concluding interpretation; detailed quantitative material may be omitted.

19
Christina D. Romer and David H. Romer, "The Evolution of Economic Understanding and Postwar Stabilization Policy" (2002)
SOURCE · NBER publication page →  ·  SOURCE · full text provided by the author →

Romer and Romer examine how policymakers' changing beliefs about potential output, inflation and aggregate demand affected actual policy. Their broader institutional lesson is important for a Board audience: serious policy errors may arise from a confidently held but incorrect interpretation of the economy rather than from a lack of commitment or analytical effort.

Recommended focus: The sections on the 1960s and 1970s and the comparison with the subsequent change in policy doctrine.

20
Thomas J. Sargent, "The Ends of Four Big Inflations" (1982)
SOURCE · NBER · chapter page and complete download →

Sargent studies the Austrian, German, Hungarian and Polish stabilisations after the First World War. Although these inflations were more extreme than the Great Inflation, the comparative cases establish that durable stabilisation may require a credible change in the broader monetary and fiscal regime, rather than an isolated interest rate decision.

Recommended focus: The comparative historical narratives and concluding synthesis; formal discussion may be omitted.

IV. Contemporary institutional relevance
21
Kenneth S. Rogoff, "Globalization and Global Disinflation" (2003)
SOURCE · International Monetary Fund · full Jackson Hole paper →

Rogoff asks whether the global fall in inflation reflected better central banking alone or also a more favourable environment created by globalisation, deregulation and competition. The paper adds an important qualification to institutional success stories: policymakers should distinguish achievements generated by the regime from favourable structural forces that may later reverse.

Recommended focus: The introduction, the global disinflation narrative and the concluding discussion of risks to the environment of low inflation. The stylised model may be omitted.

Rogoff uses the inflation surge following the pandemic to reconsider the lessons of the 1970s. The analysis bears directly on the institutional resilience required to preserve price stability.

Recommended focus: The full article.

Summers was among the earliest prominent policymakers and economists to argue that the policy mix following the pandemic created a material risk of persistent inflation, rather than a transitory change in relative prices. The article records a contemporaneous dissenting diagnosis made before the inflation outcome was known. Recommended focus: The full article, read as a contemporaneous policy argument rather than a retrospective assessment.

The essay explains how the August 1971 decision to suspend dollar convertibility to gold, alongside the first peacetime wage-price freeze in U.S. history, ended the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates. Bordo and Eichengreen argue that the acceleration of US inflation after 1965 reflected a shift in responsibility for the balance of payments away from the Federal Reserve and toward the Treasury, which freed the Fed to pursue domestic objectives more aggressively once external monetary discipline no longer constrained it. Recommended focus: The introduction and the concluding assessment of how the erosion of the gold-standard constraint changed Federal Reserve incentives; the FOMC minutes analysis may be treated as supporting evidence.

Blinder and Rudd reassess the classic supply-shock account of the 1973--74 and 1978--80 inflation spikes, attributing both largely to food and energy price shocks and, in 1973--74, the removal of Nixon-era price controls. The paper directly engages critics who assign primary responsibility to monetary policy instead, giving the Board a rigorous treatment of how much of the Great Inflation is properly attributed to supply shocks versus policy choices.

Recommended focus: The statement of the classic supply-shock explanation and the rebuttal of the monetary-policy-only view; the later discussion of why recent oil shocks have smaller effects may be omitted.

I. Institutional foundations and the design of inflation targeting
1
Frederic S. Mishkin, "International Experiences with Different Monetary Policy Regimes" (1999)
SOURCE · NBER working paper and full text →

Mishkin provides a comparative account of exchange rate pegs, monetary targeting and inflation targeting. The analysis establishes that an effective nominal anchor requires supporting institutional arrangements, public accountability and a strategy consistent with the country's economic structure and policy history.

Recommended focus: Introduction, the discussion of inflation targeting, and the concluding lessons on institutional prerequisites.

2
IMF EDITED VOLUME
Tobias Adrian, Douglas Laxton and Maurice Obstfeld, eds., "Advancing the Frontiers of Monetary Policy" (International Monetary Fund, 2018)
SOURCE · International Monetary Fund · full book →

Fourteen chapters by IMF economists synthesise a quarter-century of inflation-forecast targeting, combining first-principles chapters with country case studies including the Czech Republic, India and the United States. Several sources elsewhere in this reading list, including Governor Galstyan's own account of the CBA's framework, draw directly on the thinking developed in this volume.

Recommended focus: Chapter 1 (overview), Chapter 2 (first principles) and Chapter 14 (a robust and adaptable nominal anchor); individual country chapters may be read selectively according to Board member interest.

3
Maurice Obstfeld and Kenneth Rogoff, "The Mirage of Fixed Exchange Rates" (1995)
SOURCE · NBER working paper and full text →

Obstfeld and Rogoff explain why adjustable pegs are vulnerable when capital is mobile and policy credibility is incomplete. For a small open economy, the essay establishes the strategic case for combining exchange rate flexibility with a credible domestic nominal anchor rather than treating the exchange rate as an independent and continuously defensible objective.

Recommended focus: The conceptual argument on the fragility of soft pegs and the policy implications for open economies.

4
Guillermo A. Calvo and Frederic S. Mishkin, "The Mirage of Exchange Rate Regimes for Emerging Market Countries" (2003)
SOURCE · NBER working paper and full text →

Calvo and Mishkin shift the discussion from regime labels to the institutional conditions that determine whether a framework can endure. They emphasise fiscal discipline, financial sector soundness, institutional credibility and the capacity to withstand external shocks. The paper is therefore directly relevant to Board oversight of a monetary policy framework in an emerging market.

Recommended focus: The institutional diagnosis and the concluding recommendations for emerging market economies.

5
Andrea Schaechter, Mark R. Stone and Mark Zelmer, Adopting Inflation Targeting: Practical Issues for Emerging Market Countries (2000)
SOURCE · IMF Occasional Paper · publication page and full text →

Drawing on the experiences of Brazil, Chile, the Czech Republic, Israel, Poland and South Africa, the study examines the target, legal mandate, decision process, forecasting capacity, accountability arrangements and communications required to implement inflation targeting. It provides the principal operational reference in this section.

Recommended focus: Executive summary and the sections on institutional arrangements, target design and communications.

6
Nicoletta Batini, Kenneth Kuttner and Douglas Laxton, "Does Inflation Targeting Work in Emerging Markets?" (World Economic Outlook, Chapter 4, September 2005)
SOURCE · International Monetary Fund · World Economic Outlook chapter →

Using a large cross-country sample, the chapter finds that inflation targeting in emerging markets has been associated with lower inflation and inflation volatility without a discernible cost to output volatility, once adopted under adequate institutional preconditions. It gives the Board an accessible empirical answer to whether the framework Armenia adopted has actually worked elsewhere.

Recommended focus: The findings on emerging market performance and the discussion of preconditions for successful adoption; the technical appendix may be omitted.

7
Nicoletta Batini and Douglas Laxton, "Under What Conditions Can Inflation Targeting Be Adopted? The Experience of Emerging Markets" (2007), in Frederic S. Mishkin and Klaus Schmidt-Hebbel, eds., Monetary Policy Under Inflation Targeting (Santiago: Central Bank of Chile)
SOURCE · Central Bank of Chile · working paper version →

Batini and Laxton set out the institutional, fiscal and financial preconditions an emerging market economy should meet before adopting inflation targeting, and examine what happens when countries adopt the framework before those preconditions are fully in place. The paper complements Schaechter, Stone and Zelmer's operational account with a more analytical treatment of readiness.

Recommended focus: The statement of preconditions and the comparative evidence on early versus late adopters; the econometric sections may be omitted.

8
Laurence Ball, "The Case for a Long-Run Inflation Target of Four Percent" (IMF Working Paper 14/92, 2014)
SOURCE · International Monetary Fund · working paper in full →

Ball argues that a higher long-run inflation target than the conventional 2 percent gives a central bank more room to cut interest rates before hitting the effective lower bound, at a modest cost in terms of the distortions higher inflation itself creates. The paper gives the Board direct exposure to the live debate over the appropriate target level, relevant given the CBA's own target has been revised over time.

Recommended focus: The statement of the trade-off and the concluding assessment of the appropriate target range; the formal model may be omitted.

Bernanke and Gertler provide the foundational conceptual account of how monetary policy affects borrowing conditions through bank balance sheets, borrower net worth and the external finance premium. Frenkel's brief and foundational paper explains how openness changes the information set, constraints and instruments available to a central bank. 18
Maurice Obstfeld, "Trilemmas and Trade-offs: Living with Financial Globalisation" (2015)
SOURCE · BIS Working Paper · full text →

Obstfeld reassesses the scope for monetary autonomy when capital is mobile and global financial conditions transmit strongly across borders. The central lesson is balanced: a flexible exchange rate preserves meaningful policy space, but does not provide complete insulation from external financial shocks. Complementary macroprudential and financial policies therefore remain important.

Recommended focus: Introduction, the discussion of the monetary trilemma and the concluding policy policy choices.

Rey's Jackson Hole paper places the global financial cycle at the centre of the policy problem. The paper provides the conceptual basis for considering macroprudential measures and capital flow tools alongside the policy rate.

Recommended focus: The opening diagnosis, the policy implications and the conclusion; econometric sections may be treated as supporting material.

Recommended focus: Key takeaways and the sections on exchange rate transmission, the financial channel and the policy tool mix.

II. Monetary policy under uncertainty and the least regrets principle
7
William C. Brainard, "Uncertainty and the Effectiveness of Policy" (1967)
SOURCE · American Economic Review, Papers and Proceedings · full article →

Brainard's foundational result, now known as the Brainard conservatism principle, shows that uncertainty about the structure of the economy should generally lead a policymaker to act more cautiously rather than to disregard the uncertainty. The paper is the academic origin of the caution that the policymaker accounts later in this Part describe in practice.

Recommended focus: The statement of the central result and its policy interpretation; the formal derivation may be read selectively.

SOURCE · National Bureau of Economic Research · working paper in full →

Barro and Gordon show that a central bank with full discretion will tend toward a persistently higher inflation rate than announced, and argue that binding rules or reputational commitment are needed to overcome this bias. The paper is the classical case for rules over discretionary judgment, included so that Board members encounter the strongest counterargument to a scenario-based, discretionary framework before considering why the CBA has chosen its present approach.

Recommended focus: The statement of the discretion problem and the case for rules in the introduction and conclusion; the equilibrium derivations may be omitted.

Macroprudential Policy: Objectives, Tools and Transmission

The purpose of macroprudential policy is to preserve the financial system’s ability to provide essential services, including supply of credit and reliable functioning of payments infrastructure, when shocks occur. It does this by strengthening resilience before stress emerges, containing the build-up of vulnerabilities over the financial cycle, and addressing systemic risks.

Why financial stability matters

The financial system channels savings to investment, operates the payments and settlement infrastructure, provides credit and allows households and firms to manage risk. Financial stability exists when these services continue to function even under stress. It does not mean that asset prices never fall or that no financial institution can fail. It means that the system can absorb shocks without a broad disruption that damages economic activity.

A problem becomes systemic when it impairs all or part of the financial system and causes serious consequences for the real economy. For example, a fall in property prices can weaken collateral values and increase mortgage losses. If many banks respond by reducing lending at the same time, otherwise creditworthy households and firms may lose access to financing. Lower spending and investment then weaken incomes and balance sheets further, creating a feedback loop between the financial system and the economy.

Why institution by institution supervision is not enough

Microprudential supervision protects the safety and soundness of individual institutions and, through that, their depositors and customers. This remains essential. Macroprudential policy asks a different question: could the common behavior, exposures or linkages of otherwise sound institutions destabilize the system as a whole?

The two perspectives complement each other. Strong individual institutions make the system safer, but they do not by themselves eliminate common exposures, interconnectedness or procyclical behavior. A bank may reduce its own risk by selling assets during stress, for example, while simultaneous sales by many banks can depress prices and worsen losses across the system.

The two dimensions of systemic risk

Systemic risk is usually assessed along a time dimension and a cross-sectional or structural dimension. The distinction guides the choice and calibration of instruments.

DimensionWhat policymakers examineTypical policy response
Time dimensionHow credit, leverage, funding fragility and asset prices build up and unwind over the financial cycleBuild buffers and restrain excessive risk-taking during expansions; release usable buffers when stress threatens credit provision
Structural dimensionHow risk is distributed across institutions, sectors and infrastructures at a point in timeApply stronger requirements to systemically important institutions and limit concentration, common exposures or interconnectedness

The time dimension reflects procyclicality. During an expansion, strong collateral values, low measured defaults and optimistic expectations may encourage more lending and leverage. These developments can make risk appear low precisely when vulnerabilities are accumulating. When conditions reverse, losses, tighter credit and asset sales may reinforce one another. The structural dimension captures the possibility that the failure or distress of an important institution, a common exposure, or a critical financial infrastructure can transmit losses through the system.

Objectives of macroprudential policy

The ultimate objective of macroprudential policy is to safeguard financial stability, that is, to mitigate risks that threaten financial stability. Collectively, these risks are referred to as systemic risk, and addressing systemic risk is the primary objective of macroprudential policy. Systemic risk is the risk of disruption to the normal functioning of the financial system as a whole or a significant part of it, with potentially serious adverse consequences for the current and future functioning of the real economy. Systemic risk has two dimensions: a time dimension and a structural dimension.

The intermediate objectives, both internationally accepted and applied in Armenia, are to:

  • mitigate and prevent excessive credit growth and leverage;
  • mitigate and prevent excessive maturity mismatch and market illiquidity;
  • limit direct and indirect exposure concentrations;
  • limit the systemic impact of misaligned incentives with a view to reducing moral hazard; and
  • strengthen the resilience of financial infrastructures.

The macroprudential toolkit

Macroprudential tools can be grouped by whether they primarily affect lenders, borrowers or system-wide liquidity and exposures. Most tools strengthen resilience; some also affect the pace or composition of credit. Their effect depends on calibration, the phase of the financial cycle, bank and borrower responses, and possible circumvention.

How the main tools transmit

Capital based tools

Capital buffers increase the amount of bank funding that can absorb losses before creditors are affected. When a buffer is raised, banks can meet it by retaining earnings, issuing capital or adjusting the growth and composition of assets. The immediate effect may include somewhat higher lending costs or slower balance-sheet growth. The main intended effect, however, is greater capacity to withstand future losses.

The countercyclical capital buffer is specifically designed to vary with cyclical systemic risk. Authorities can increase it while vulnerabilities are building, giving banks time to accumulate usable capital. If risk materializes and credit supply is threatened, the buffer can be released. Release does not compel banks to lend, but it removes a regulatory constraint and allows existing capital to absorb losses without forcing an equally large contraction in assets. A positive-neutral CCyB can create releasable policy space before risks become visibly elevated.

Borrower based tools

Borrower-based limits apply to the terms on which new loans are originated, although lenders implement them. An LTV cap limits the mortgage relative to the value of the property. With a 90 percent cap, for example, the borrower normally provides at least 10 percent of the property’s value from other funds. A DSTI cap limits required debt payments relative to income, while a DTI cap limits the stock of debt relative to income.

These instruments improve the resilience of both borrowers and lenders. More borrower equity reduces the probability that a mortgage becomes larger than the value of its collateral after a price decline. A lower debt-service burden leaves more room to absorb a fall in income or a rise in interest rates. The limits can also moderate credit and housing demand, but they may reduce access to credit, especially for liquidity-constrained first-time buyers. Their design therefore often includes differentiated limits, exemptions or speed limits, which must be transparent and monitored.

Liquidity and exposure tools

Liquidity requirements reduce reliance on funding that may disappear quickly under stress. The liquidity coverage ratio requires a stock of high-quality liquid assets against short-term net cash outflows. The net stable funding ratio encourages a more stable funding structure over a longer horizon. Foreign currency rules can address the additional risk created when assets and liabilities are denominated in different currencies or when funding is concentrated in foreign currency deposits.

Exposure and concentration limits prevent a single borrower, sector, counterparty or funding source from creating losses large enough to threaten the system. Structural capital surcharges for systemically important institutions recognize that the distress of a large, interconnected or difficult-to-substitute institution would impose greater costs on the economy and may create moral hazard.

From risk assessment to a policy decision

Macroprudential policy is a continuous process rather than a mechanical reaction to one indicator. The CBA’s implementation framework follows a sequence: monitor systemic risks; identify and measure their sources; select an intermediate objective and instrument; calibrate the instrument; communicate and implement the decision; and assess effectiveness with possible recalibration.

Authorities combine indicators, models and expert judgement. Relevant evidence can include credit growth and the credit-to-GDP gap, debt-service burdens, lending standards, property prices, loan defaults, capital and liquidity positions, funding concentration, interconnectedness and market-based measures. A broad and consistent set of signals provides a stronger basis for action than a single threshold.

Stress testing is an analytical tool, not itself a macroprudential instrument. It examines whether institutions and the system can withstand severe but plausible scenarios. The CBA conducts top-down solvency and liquidity stress tests for the banking system. The results help assess resilience and inform the calibration of macroprudential tools.

Timing creates an unavoidable trade-off. Tightening too early or too strongly can unnecessarily restrict credit, while acting too late allows vulnerabilities to grow. During stress, releasing buffers too early may weaken resilience if risks continue to build; releasing them too late may intensify a credit contraction. For this reason, many authorities use guided discretion: indicators organize the assessment, while the final decision considers the full risk environment and the expected transmission of the tool.

Macroprudential policy in Armenia

The Constitution of the Republic of Armenia and the Law on the Central Bank identify financial stability, alongside price stability, as a core objective of the CBA. The CBA combines system-wide risk monitoring, prudential regulation, supervision, stress testing and communication in its financial stability framework. This is especially important because banks account for the largest part of Armenia’s financial system.

The CBA adopted the framework governing capital-based macroprudential instruments in 2019 and makes CCyB decisions quarterly. In 2023, it introduced the concept of a neutral CCyB: a positive buffer maintained in a normal phase of the financial cycle, when cyclical risks are neither elevated nor subdued. The approach creates capital that can be released if an unexpected shock threatens the supply of credit.

This example illustrates the forward-looking nature of policy. The absence of widespread defaults does not imply the absence of systemic risk. Macroprudential assessment considers whether current credit, leverage, funding and asset-price dynamics could make the system vulnerable when conditions change.

Interaction with monetary policy

Monetary and macroprudential policies influence some of the same variables, including credit conditions, asset prices and risk-taking, but they have different primary objectives. Monetary policy uses broad financial conditions to maintain price stability and stabilize the macroeconomy. Macroprudential policy encompasses range of targeted instruments in its toolkit enabling it to address the diverse range of vulnerabilities in the financial system directly and effectively.

The policies can reinforce each other. A resilient banking system improves the transmission of monetary policy, and stable inflation supports sound financial decisions. Tensions are also possible. Higher interest rates can weaken debt-service capacity, while tight borrower-based measures can alter the effect of monetary policy on housing and credit. Under normal conditions, monetary and macroprudential policies operate independently while maintaining full information sharing, with closer policy coordination becoming necessary during periods of financial stress. Macroprudential tools, however, should not be loosened merely because aggregate demand weakens. The relevant question is whether systemic risk has receded or materialized and whether financial stress threatens the continued provision of credit and other services.

Inflation Target in Armenia

A reliable and clearly communicated inflation target is an important benchmark for businesses in decision-making, setting contracts, and forming inflation expectations. Over time, the Central Bank may consider it necessary and appropriate to revise the inflation target to best reflect the long-term structural characteristics of the economy, its driving forces, and the vision for the country’s future economic development and improvement of the country’s wellbeing.

Modern economic literature asserts that an inflation target of zero is the best and most optimal medium-term inflation target that is consistent with price stability. However, the literature also identifies a number of arguments that can explain why a low positive target is chosen in practice. These include factors relating to the zero lower bound of the nominal interest rate, downward rigidities for prices of factors of production (especially wages), and statistical errors in calculating inflation. Taking these factors into account, a low, positive inflation target is considered an acceptable and optimal solution.

Since the adoption of the inflation targeting strategy in 2006, the Central Bank has consistently achieved the proposed 4% inflation target. As such, between 2006 and 2026, the average year-over-year rate in Armenia has stood at approximately 3.9% (Figure 1). This is the most important indicator of the success and effectiveness of the CBA’s monetary policy. However, it is important to note that this has not fully translated to the public perceiving price stability, as during various regional and internal shocks, the Armenian economy often faced risks of inflation and deanchored inflation expectations. The prevalence of similar situations over the course of Armenia’s monetary policy history may offer signals that the 4% target may currently not be consistent with the definition of true price stability, or that maintaining price stability in line with a 4% target may come at the expense of significant social costs.

Throughout the implementation of the inflation targeting strategy, the CBA Armenia has made significant investments in developing the Forecasting and Policy Analysis System (FPAS) for monetary policy. Investments in the FPAS over time have laid a solid foundation for implementing wide-ranging reforms in this direction in the present period. Against this background, in 2024, the Central Bank of Armenia officially introduced an innovative and progressive approach to monetary policy aimed at prudently managing risks and uncertainties.

In recent decades, Armenia's economy and economic situation have improved significantly, becoming more robust, diversified, and globally interconnected. Parallel to this economic growth and development, the central bank’s framework and strategy for price stability and monetary policy have also undergone fundamental changes and developed in several stages, providing a stronger and more robust foundation for prudently managing a growing layering of risks and uncertainties. In recent years, the Armenian economy has benefitted from consistent structural and institutional improvements, while the financial system has made significant strides in becoming more developed and robust, whether in terms of its depth, diversity of sectors, or significant expansion of its toolkit. It is also worth mentioning that with the successes achieved in the fields of monetary and macroeconomic policy as well as in developing robust policymaking frameworks, Armenia is currently “classified” by international experts and investors as a country with a leading monetary policy framework. Therefore, setting a lower and consistent inflation target is also an important factor from the perspective of Armenia’s international reputation and rating.

Extensive global experience in the implementation of monetary policy and the available research confirm that ensuring low inflation significantly reduces uncertainties associated with future monetary policy and price developments, contributing to the reduction of volatility in the pricing process and creating a favorable environment for sustainable economic growth. This is due to the increased predictability of investment and savings results in a low-inflation and low-volatility environment — which are positively correlated (Figure 2) — the extension of investment terms, as well as the formation of relatively low nominal interest rates. In a low-inflation environment, firms and households do not need to incur various costs to avoid inflation or to effectively plan for consumption and savings. In this context, it can be argued that ensuring low stable inflation is not an end in itself and is an important guarantee of long-term sustainable economic growth. As the analysis of various countries’ high economic growth situations during different time periods shows, if we filter out the situations that ended with instability and economic crises, then stable high economic growth was usually accompanied by the development of low — on average 3% — inflation developments. It is no coincidence that, in most countries, the goal of price stability delegated to central banks is considered as the basis for societal welfare and/or long-term sustainable economic growth.

Fiscal Policy and Its Main Instruments

Fiscal policy is the set of measures through which the government influences the economy by managing public revenues, expenditures, the budget deficit, and public debt. Alongside monetary policy, it is one of the main macroeconomic policies that influence aggregate demand, economic activity, and inflation.

The main instruments of fiscal policy are government spending and revenue collection. An increase in government spending or a reduction in the tax burden, all else being equal, can increase aggregate demand and have an expansionary effect, whereas more moderate growth in spending or an increase in the tax burden can have a contractionary effect. At the same time, the impact of fiscal policy depends not only on the overall level of spending but also on its composition, whether it is temporary or permanent, and the economy's cyclical position.

Government spending is generally divided into current and capital expenditures. Current expenditures include, for example, public-sector wages, social benefits and pensions, purchases of goods and services, subsidies, and interest payments. Capital expenditures are primarily directed toward infrastructure and other public investment programs. This distinction is important for the Central Bank because, for example, an increase in wages and social transfers can be transmitted relatively quickly to household consumption, whereas effective capital expenditures, in addition to their short-term impact on demand, can also increase the economy's productive capacity over the medium term.

The State Budget and the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework

The State Budget is the government's main annual financial plan, which sets out public revenues, expenditures, the budget deficit, and its sources of financing. It reflects the government's planned fiscal policy for the given year. From the perspective of scenario building and fiscal policy analysis, however, it is important to distinguish between the approved budget and its actual execution. For example, if major capital expenditures planned in the budget are not carried out during the year or are postponed to a subsequent period, the actual fiscal impulse transmitted to the economy may differ significantly from what was initially expected.

The Medium-Term Expenditure Framework (MTEF) places the annual budget within a medium-term five-year framework and presents the government's key macroeconomic and fiscal projections, expenditure priorities, and medium-term fiscal trajectory. Thus, the State Budget primarily answers the question "What is planned to be implemented this year?", while the MTEF addresses "What direction is fiscal policy taking over the medium term?"

Both are important for monetary policy, since the current inflationary impact largely depends on the actual execution of the budget, while understanding the fiscal stance in the following years is also necessary when assessing medium-term effects and the redistribution of revenues collected from the economy.

The Budget Deficit, Public Debt, and Fiscal Rules

The budget deficit is the difference between government revenues and expenditures over a given period and is a flow measure. The deficit is financed through domestic or external borrowing and other sources of financing. Public debt, by contrast, is a stock measure and reflects the level of government debt accumulated over time.

The size of the deficit alone does not fully capture the impact of fiscal policy on the economy. The same deficit can have different effects depending on the reasons behind its occurrence, the composition of expenditures, the economy's cyclical position, and the method of financing. For example, the short- and medium-term effects of a deficit driven by capital investment may differ from those of a deficit of the same size resulting from increases in wages or other current expenditures.

The level of public debt is also important because it affects the government's fiscal space, interest expenditures, financing conditions, and its ability to respond to shocks. When debt levels are high, the scope for future fiscal policy may be more limited, while a deterioration in public financing conditions can be transmitted to the broader financial conditions of the economy.

The size of the deficit alone does not capture fiscal impact. What matters is its cause, composition, and how it is financed.

Box 1 · Armenia's Fiscal Rules

In Armenia, fiscal policy is constrained by fiscal rules that set limits on the budget balance, public debt, and expenditures. The maximum budget deficit is set at 7.5% of GDP, while the key thresholds for government debt are 40%, 50%, and 60% of GDP.

When public debt exceeds 40% of GDP, capital expenditures must not be lower than the budget deficit. When debt exceeds 50% of GDP, a restriction is imposed on the growth of current primary expenditures. When debt exceeds 60% of GDP, the restrictions become more stringent, and an appropriate debt-reduction program is required. The existing framework also provides for the possibility of temporarily deviating from the fiscal rules in exceptional circumstances.

From the Central Bank's perspective, fiscal rules are important not only for assessing debt sustainability; they also define the medium-term constraints within which the government can adjust expenditures and the budget deficit. Therefore, the level of public debt can affect the future fiscal impulse, the government's ability to respond to economic shocks, and, consequently, the required monetary policy stance.

Fiscal Impulse

From a monetary policy perspective, the key consideration is the change in the fiscal stance and its impact on aggregate demand. This impact is generally described as the fiscal impulse.

To assess the fiscal impulse, it is important to consider not only changes in the budget deficit but also the individual developments in revenues and expenditures, their composition, and their changes relative to the previous period. For example, the budget may remain in deficit, while the reduction in the deficit may be driven either by more moderate growth in expenditures or by strong growth in tax revenues, with the two having different effects on aggregate demand. Similarly, an increase in the deficit may be driven by an expansion in expenditures or by a decline in tax revenues resulting from weaker economic activity. Therefore, assessing the fiscal impulse aims to determine the extent to which changes in revenues and expenditures, taken together, add to or withdraw demand from the economy.

At the same time, it is necessary to distinguish automatic changes in budgetary indicators resulting from the economic cycle from the government's discretionary policy decisions. For example, an increase in tax revenues resulting from strong economic growth does not, in itself, indicate a tightening of fiscal policy. Therefore, the purpose of assessing the fiscal impulse is to determine the extent to which changes in government policy actually create or withdraw additional demand from the economy.

Fiscal Policy and Monetary Policy

Fiscal policy primarily affects inflation through aggregate demand. An increase in government spending, wages, or social transfers can boost consumption, while public investment can increase demand in the construction and related sectors. Its impact on the labor market can also be transmitted to wages in the private sector.

This impact depends on the economy's position in the business cycle. If there is spare productive capacity in the economy, a significant portion of additional demand may translate into higher real output. If the economy is operating close to or above its potential, the same increase in demand may instead result in greater inflationary pressures. In a small open economy, part of the additional demand may also be directed toward imports, affecting the current account and foreign exchange flows.

In this context, fiscal and monetary policy stances can either complement or counteract each other. If fiscal policy provides additional stimulus to demand amid inflationary pressures, a tighter or more prolonged monetary policy stance may be required to bring inflation back to its target. Conversely, when demand is weak, appropriate fiscal support can work in the same direction as monetary policy. Coordination between fiscal and monetary policies does not imply the integration of their policy objectives or decision-making processes. For the Central Bank, it primarily means incorporating comprehensive information on the government's programs, budget execution, financing, and their macroeconomic effects into scenario development and monetary policy decisions, while maintaining the Central Bank's institutional independence and its price stability objective.

Bottom line: Fiscal policy is not a background variable that monetary policy simply forecasts around. Its composition, its cyclical versus discretionary drivers, and the credibility of the medium-term path all shape the fiscal impulse the economy actually receives, and therefore the monetary policy stance needed to keep inflation at target. Coordination does not mean shared objectives or shared decision-making; it means the Central Bank incorporating comprehensive, timely information on the government's fiscal programs and their macroeconomic effects, while preserving its own institutional independence and price stability mandate.

The Neutral Interest Rate: The Concept and Its Use in Policy Decisions

What the Neutral Rate Means for Policy

The neutral rate of interest is the real interest rate that would prevail in the absence of business-cycle fluctuations, when output is at potential, saving is equal to investment, inflation is stable, and the economy is operating at full employment. In other words, it is the level of interest rate that is consistent with aggregate demand and aggregate supply being in equilibrium. From the perspective of monetary policy, a policy rate below this benchmark is, other things equal, expansionary; a rate above it is contractionary. The difficulty is that the neutral rate cannot be observed directly. Just as with variables like potential output and the natural rate of unemployment, the neutral interest rate is an unobservable variable, meaning that policymakers cannot directly observe or quantify it with a degree of certainty, and as a result, there is always fundamental uncertainty surrounding estimates of the neutral rate at any point in time. Further complicating this uncertainty are the nonlinearities that can arise from policy errors associated with incorrectly estimating the neutral rate. While a slight over- or under-estimation of the neutral rate may not have significant policy implications, vast misjudgments in the level of the neutral rate may have significant and nonlinear policy implications, steering the economy toward “dark corners” of de-anchored inflation expectations or a low inflation trap.

The unobservable nature of the neutral rate complicates its precise estimation. It must instead be inferred from a range of economic indicators and assumptions about the underlying forces shaping saving, investment, productivity, demographics, and potential output. This makes estimates of the neutral rate inherently uncertain and subject to considerable judgment. Robert S. Kaplan and Larry Summers have used the analogy of driving a vehicle to illustrate this point. Much like a driver judging speed and road conditions in real time, with little foresight of what lies beyond the curve ahead, policymakers must make decisions based on an imperfect sense of where the neutral rate lies. However, the fact that the signal is imprecise does not make it superfluous. Indeed, as Summers argues, policymakers cannot simply “drive on feel” without attempting to gauge the neutral rate; doing so would risk losing an important reference point for determining the appropriate stance of monetary policy. The challenge, therefore, is not to identify the neutral rate with precision, but to develop sufficiently informed and robust estimates to guide policy decisions under uncertainty.

“We cannot identify with certainty the neutral rate of interest … as is often the case, we are navigating by the stars under cloudy skies.”
Jerome Powell, 2018

“Saying ‘we don’t need to know what the neutral rate is’ is like saying you should drive your car on feel, without looking at the speedometer. It is just a mistake.”
Larry Summers, 2024, arguing that uncertainty does not make the neutral rate irrelevant

For policymaking, it is useful to distinguish the long-run neutral rate from the shorter-run inflation-stabilizing real interest rate. The long-run rate is the equilibrium real rate expected to prevail once temporary shocks have faded and the business cycle is accounted for, and therefore moves with structural forces such as productivity, saving and investment, fiscal conditions, and the sovereign risk premium. The shorter-run rate can move around that anchor when current shocks alter demand or financial conditions. For Armenia, this distinction matters because the two horizons can move differently. A durable decline in the sovereign risk premium could lower the long-run neutral rate, while strong domestic demand or credit growth could keep the short-run inflation-stabilizing rate temporarily higher.

Neutral Interest Rates in the United States

Roughly speaking, the neutral rate in the United States is the benchmark upon which neutral rates in other economies are determined. However, considerable uncertainty surrounds estimates of the US neutral rate. Assumptions about the state of the underlying economic factors that drive neutral rates vary widely and require a heavy dose of judgment, and as a result, measures of the neutral rate can vary considerably depending on the source. For example, the Federal Reserve’s long-run policy rate projections stand at 2.5%; netting out the 2% inflation target suggests a long-run real neutral rate of around 0.5 percent. The well-regarded Holston-Laubach-Williams measure, meanwhile, stands a little higher, at 0.7 percent. Alternatively, one can look to markets for insights, which suggest a range between 1.0 and 1.5 percent, depending on assumptions regarding the 5-year forward TIPS rate for liquidity premiums. The fact that markets, as well as several leading economists such as Larry Summers, are estimating a higher neutral rate relative to where central banks and some of these R-star measures stand suggests that careful attention should be paid to understanding the uncertainty about the fundamentals that drive neutral rates, in order to develop sensible estimates that mitigate the likelihood of policy errors stemming from incorrect judgments.

Several factors drive the level of the neutral rate, including productivity, demographics, global capital flows, and regulations, and there is considerable uncertainty surrounding estimates for each of these factors. Over the past two decades, the US neutral rate has declined substantially, from around 3.0–3.5% to somewhere between 0.5 and 1.0% by the late 2010s. This gradual decline was likely driven by a confluence of factors, including falling productivity; demographic factors such as aging, where higher life expectancy and slower productivity growth require greater saving for retirement; and the global savings glut, particularly driven by China and other Asian countries. This slow and long-lasting slump in the neutral rate brought about a low-neutral-rate view of the world, in which equilibrium real returns were largely assumed to remain low “forever.” From the perspective of policy, the main risks and fears related to “secular stagnation” were not about high or persistent inflation, but rather the dangers of being stuck at the effective lower bound and lacking conventional policy tools to stimulate demand during economic contractions. This also drove fears of a very low deflationary environment, or deflation trap, and of inflation expectations becoming de-anchored below target, further restricting growth.

However, the Covid crisis shook many of these Great Moderation-era assumptions to their foundations, and once again brought discussions about higher neutral rates back to the center of public discourse. Successive supply shocks and bottlenecks, coupled with a faster-than-expected recovery of demand on the heels of unprecedentedly expansionary fiscal and monetary policies, brought about a period of high inflation and robust consumption that continues to persist. Despite the continued tightening of the policy stance by the Fed to levels not seen in decades, domestic demand, consumption, and labor market conditions in the US have remained stubbornly elevated, driving persistently high inflation with no obvious, sustainable path back to the inflation target. The fact that a seemingly restrictive policy stance has thus far been insufficient in establishing durable price stability may suggest that the short-term neutral rate has drifted higher than what the Federal Reserve has estimated it to be. More recently, higher tariffs and renewed trade tensions have added to goods-price pressures, while the US-Iran conflict and broader Middle East tensions have generated additional pressures through energy prices, transportation costs, and global supply chains. In an environment of resilient demand conditions, successive adverse supply shocks pose a much more significant inflationary threat, in turn requiring higher interest rates to be sufficiently restrictive to establish price stability. The impact is likely not limited to the short-term interest rate; several factors suggest that the long-term equilibrium level of interest rates has also increased. One of the major factors driving a higher long-term equilibrium neutral rate is rising government debt in advanced economies. Net debt-to-GDP ratios in OECD countries have nearly doubled since the Global Financial Crisis, from 39% in 2007 to over 75% in 2025. Most advanced economies continue to run budget deficits, with government expenditures consistently outpacing revenues; the US government debt level famously crested the $40 trillion mark in August 2026. Worryingly, there does not appear to be a politically viable solution to fiscal instability in sight, as governments are hesitant either to cut back spending, which would strip key benefits such as welfare and social insurance, or to raise revenues by increasing taxes on households or businesses. As governments rely on deficit spending and increase their debt levels, government saving naturally declines; because the private saving rate cannot rise as much as government saving declines, the net global saving rate in turn falls, requiring higher global interest rates to restore equilibrium.1 Beyond government debt, other factors are also generating upward pressure on interest rates. The rapid expansion of AI-related investment, which is incredibly capital-intensive, has provided an additional source of demand for capital, in turn raising the return investors seek on their investments. Sustained investment in defense, energy security, and supply-chain resilience, in a global environment where geopolitical tensions are becoming increasingly inflamed and countries are placing greater emphasis on sovereignty in national security, energy, and supply chains, could also generate upward pressure. Acknowledging these developments could result in a “landing point” for interest rates that is higher than what markets have priced in as of today. These saving and investment dynamics are, in many ways, the opposite of the factors that drove neutral rates down during the Great Moderation.

While both short- and long-term neutral rates have most likely increased in recent years, their future developments remain quite uncertain. The recent upward pressures described above may prove significant enough to outweigh the forces that had been, and are likely to continue, pulling the neutral rate down over the longer term. Further trade fragmentation, geopolitical escalation, continued fiscal pressures, and strong investment demand could reinforce the upward shift, and may not yet be fully reflected in financial markets. These considerations point to the dominance of upside risks for the future trajectory of neutral rates. These risks are further compounded by uncertainty about the term structure, where upward shifts in the term premium could meaningfully drive up long-term market rates without necessarily representing an equivalent increase in r*.2

Naturally, the uncertainty surrounding its proper measurement poses challenges for policymakers. As Fed Chair Powell noted, navigating by the stars is challenging, especially “under cloudy skies.” While neutral rates can serve as a helpful tool to understand and illustrate key considerations about economic conditions, the key concern is not necessarily about finding the perfect estimate to dictate policy actions, but rather about having sensible frameworks in place to think about and communicate the uncertainty around neutral rates and the fundamentals driving them. Here, the prudent risk management framework is especially useful, providing policymakers with a systematic approach not just for considering this uncertainty, but for making policy decisions amid that uncertainty that seek to avoid losses stemming from a neutral-rate-related policy error in either direction.

Neutral Interest Rates in Armenia

In Armenia, as with other small, open, emerging economies, the neutral interest rate can be affected by a number of factors driven by domestic and global economic fundamentals, both short and long term in nature, around which there also exists considerable uncertainty. Moreover, given the difficulty associated with quantifying each of these factors with a degree of certain exactitude, it becomes particularly important to understand the dynamics affecting their movement and the factors that underlie them.

Global neutral real interest rate. One of the most important factors influencing the neutral rate in Armenia is the neutral rate globally, and specifically in the United States, given its central role in the global economy in terms of financial flows, capital markets, and the role of the US dollar in global markets. The uncertainties surrounding the neutral rate in the United States are described in the previous section, but the general consensus is that developments since 2020 are generating meaningful upward pressure on the long-term neutral rate.

Armenia’s sovereign risk premium. The sovereign risk premium represents the additional return that investors demand in order to hold a specific government’s bonds, compensating for the risk that the nation might default. Several factors affect the sovereign risk premium, both fundamental and cyclical. Fundamental factors relate to the country’s macroeconomic fundamentals: fiscal stability, the credibility of its monetary, macroprudential, and fiscal policymakers in maintaining macroeconomic stability, and the level of geopolitical risk and vulnerability, among others. Cyclical factors stem from the global financial cycle, particularly toward the end of the cycle, when investors are willing to accept a lower spread on bonds from traditionally higher-risk countries in the search for higher yield. A central uncertainty is the extent to which movements in the spread demanded by investors relative to a benchmark or risk-free rate are driven by country fundamentals or by short-term factors related to the global financial cycle. Armenia’s sovereign risk premium has declined considerably since 2025, reflecting a mix of global and country-specific drivers. On the one hand, long-term structural reforms in emerging market economies overall, and especially in Armenia, have increased emerging markets’ attractiveness relative to advanced economies, many of which have entrenched problems with fiscal sustainability and government debt; weakening geopolitical tensions and prospects of unblocking regional transport links have contributed further to a fundamental decline in the country risk premium. On the other hand, short-term factors related to the global financial cycle have led investors to seek yields in emerging and frontier markets, and the fact that spreads have narrowed between countries with varying sovereign credit ratings suggests that cyclical factors may also be at play. The main uncertainty relates to the extent to which current trends in emerging markets are driven by long-term, fundamental factors or by cyclical, reversal-prone ones, which in turn creates significant uncertainty regarding the long-term, stable level of Armenia’s country risk premium and the interpretation of the neutral interest rate. If policymakers assume the decline in the risk premium to be cyclical when it is actually fundamental, this could result in an unnecessarily tight policy stance that unduly punishes the real economy; if policymakers assume it is fundamental in nature but it proves to be cyclical, the loss associated with a reversal could be significant. This has been a key topic of Board deliberations during this period, and the CBA’s Transparency Reports capture the varying interpretations well. In the June 2026 policy decision, several Board members viewed the continued decline in country risk as consistent with a lower neutral rate and therefore a more restrictive prevailing policy stance, while also emphasizing that a reversal in global liquidity or weaker regional or fiscal fundamentals could cause the risk premium to be reassessed.

Equilibrium real exchange rate and productive capacity. A stronger dram does not automatically mean a lower neutral rate. The key question is whether the appreciation is cyclical or reflects a more fundamentally appreciated equilibrium exchange rate. Since 2022, Armenia has experienced significant changes in external demand, capital and labor inflows, the structure of services exports, and productive capacity. The growing role of more productive, exportable sectors, together with capital accumulation and expanding export capacity, may support stronger trend appreciation of the real exchange rate. If these developments represent a lasting improvement in Armenia’s economic fundamentals, the economy may be operating around a different equilibrium, in which a more appreciated real exchange rate is sustainable and the dram-denominated neutral rate is lower. If, instead, the appreciation largely reflects temporary flows and cyclical factors, it provides much weaker evidence of a lower long-run neutral rate and may eventually reverse.

Productivity growth. Growth in productivity results in higher marginal returns on capital, driving greater investment demand and increases in the equilibrium rate. In Armenia, developments in recent years, including the emergence of highly productive sectors such as tech, structural shifts in the economy from less productive sectors such as agriculture toward more productive sectors such as manufacturing and some services, and greater fiscal performance with respect to capital expenditure that can raise the marginal return on capital, attest to growth in productivity that could result in relatively higher neutral rates. However, given how exportable these sectors are, this could be offset by appreciation in real exchange rate trend expectations, as discussed above.

Longer-term trends and developments. Over a long-term horizon, a number of other factors may also affect the equilibrium interest rate. These include demographic shifts, where an increasingly aging population with higher life expectancy may motivate a higher saving rate, prompting downward pressure on the neutral rate. Longer-term changes in patterns of consumption, known as the propensity for intertemporal substitution of consumption, where people prefer to consume less and save more today, particularly as incomes and wealth grow, may similarly push longer-term decreases in the neutral rate.

Inflation expectations and the nominal anchor. The final channel is conceptually different, because it maps the real neutral rate into the nominal rate seen by the Board and the public. All else equal, the reduction in the inflation target from 4% to 3% in 2025 should result in a corresponding decline in the long-term neutral rate in Armenia. The short-term neutral rate, however, may be affected by fluctuations in inflation expectations in response to economic conditions: when inflation expectations increase, the short-term neutral rate logically rises, as a higher policy rate is necessary to bring inflation sustainably back to target. This question is complicated, however, by the fact that inflation expectations are notoriously difficult to measure. The CBA relies on a number of measures, including its own household survey as well as assessments of underlying inflation indicators such as wage growth and non-traded sticky-price inflation.

Board-Level Interpretation

The most difficult practical step is consolidating these divergent views. Armenia has one policy rate, but an eight-member Board can hold several internally consistent views of the neutral rate. One member may put more weight on a structurally higher global real rate; another may see the fall in Armenia’s sovereign risk premium as persistent; another may interpret the post-2022 appreciation of the dram as evidence of a different equilibrium external position. Even if all members start from the same data, their neutral-rate assessments can differ because they attach different probabilities, persistence, and policy significance to the same developments.

This is not unique to Armenia. The dispersion in the FOMC’s longer-run rate projections illustrates that policymakers with access to the same institutional information can still arrive at materially different views of neutral. The objective of a committee should therefore not be to average away those differences too quickly; the more useful objective is to make each view traceable, identifying which component is driving the assessment, whether the member believes the change is temporary or structural, and what future evidence would strengthen or weaken that narrative. Two Board members can agree on today’s policy rate while holding very different views about why it is appropriate and where policy may need to move next. As Alan Taylor has put it, there is value for central banks in communicating policymakers’ beliefs about the neutral interest rate, whether the view is collective or individual.

The CBA framework does not require uncertainty around the neutral rate to be resolved into a single Board estimate. Different assessments of its underlying components can instead coexist within the policymaking process, where they are discussed, challenged, and translated into policy-relevant Case A- and Case B-type scenarios. A key feature of the framework is that these differences do not disappear once the policy decision is made. The CBA’s Transparency Report publishes attributed Board deliberations, individual voting records, and one-paragraph vote explanations, allowing external audiences to see the assumptions and risk assessments that underpin each member’s decision. For an unobservable variable such as the neutral rate, this is especially important: the public can understand whether a member’s assessment reflects a different view of the US neutral rate, Armenia’s country risk premium, the equilibrium exchange rate, or inflation expectations, and how that view affects the perceived degree of monetary policy restrictiveness. By linking alternative assessments to explicit assumptions, and carrying those assumptions through attributed Board deliberations and individual vote explanations, the framework allows markets and the public to understand how each member interprets the stance of monetary policy, which risks they place greater weight on, and what developments could lead that assessment to change. In this way, differing views become an informative part of policy communication rather than a source of ambiguity.
